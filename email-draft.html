Does spectral gradient filtering reduce noise when training on financial time-series data?

Autonomous AI Safety Research Report · 2 August 2026 · Original idea

What We Investigated

A prior project built a "Spectral Optimizer": a wrapper around the standard AdamW optimizer that computes the gradient of the loss for each training sample individually, builds the matrix of similarities between those per-sample gradients, and keeps only the update directions that many samples agree on — using a threshold from random matrix theory (the Marchenko–Pastur law, which says how large a similarity eigenvalue must be before it is distinguishable from pure noise). Its strongest prior result was label-noise robustness: on MNIST digits with 90% of labels randomised, it reached ~80% accuracy where Adam reached ~37%.

This run asked whether that noise-filtering mechanism transfers to a genuinely hard low-signal domain: predicting financial returns. We tested whether the filter improves out-of-sample performance over a properly tuned AdamW baseline on the Numerai v5 tournament dataset (a large, public, deliberately obfuscated financial dataset with time-period-based evaluation), and whether any effect is consistent across two architectures — a plain multilayer perceptron (MLP) and a recurrent network (GRU). Understanding when gradient-consensus filtering helps and when it does not matters for building training methods that are robust to noisy data, and the outcome was genuinely uncertain in both directions: the prior work itself found the filter hurts when the useful signal is weak, which is exactly the financial regime.

Bottom Line

Negative result — and a well-explained one. The spectral filter showed no evidence of benefit, and at the pre-registered operating point it significantly degraded out-of-sample performance on both architectures. Two mechanistic findings explain why: (1) a mathematical proof that for this loss type the filter is inherently blind to the prediction targets, so it cannot be selecting "signal" directions; and (2) matched controls showing the filter's supposedly clever eigenvalue-based selection performs statistically the same as projecting onto a random subspace of equal size. The label-noise success story does not transfer to low-signal financial regression.

Approach

We reused the existing Spectral Optimizer code (per the original brief — not a reimplementation) and evaluated it on Numerai v5 data under a fully pre-registered protocol: hyperparameters were tuned on one block of time periods ("eras") and the final verdict was computed on a later, strictly separated block, with an embargo gap between them to prevent leakage. The comparison was the filter switched on vs. off around an identically tuned AdamW baseline, run on both an MLP and a GRU with multiple random seeds. Crucially, the design included a control arm that projects gradients onto a random subspace matched in size and update magnitude to what the spectral filter selects — this isolates whether the eigenvalue-based selection itself does anything beyond generic projection. All experiments ran on free-partition L40 GPUs on the MATS Slurm cluster (~15 minutes total, plus ~9 minutes of audit re-execution).

The headline metric is mean per-era correlation: within each time period, the correlation between the model's predictions and the true returns (Numerai's standard score), averaged across the 255 held-out evaluation eras. Higher is better; the tuned baseline scored about +0.0064, so effects of a few thousandths are large in relative terms. Uncertainty is quantified with a moving-block bootstrap, a confidence-interval method that respects the fact that adjacent time periods are correlated.

Why It Failed

Two findings jointly close the case. First, the target-independence proof: for a single-output model trained with mean-squared error, each sample's gradient is just ± (the sign of its prediction error) times a direction that depends only on the input features — not the targets. So the similarity structure the filter eigendecomposes is exactly the same under any shuffling of the targets (verified numerically to machine precision, ~10−16). The filter cannot be finding "directions where samples agree about the signal", because its selection never sees the signal. Second, the random-subspace control: when you match the filter's update size and dimensionality but pick the directions at random, performance is statistically identical on both architectures. The prior label-noise success evidently worked through a mechanism (multi-class classification gradients do depend on labels) that simply does not exist in this regression setting.

One honest caveat on the headline: the tuning budget was compute-matched overall, which left the spectral arm only ~1 effective tuning trial vs. 12 for the baseline. The pre-registered claim was therefore downgraded to "no evidence of benefit under the affordable tuning budget", with the raw degradation numbers reported alongside rather than claimed as the method's ceiling.

Surprising Findings

  1. A planned sanity check failed by proof rather than by noise. The diagnostic comparing the filter's behaviour on real vs. shuffled targets came back exactly identical — leading to the discovery that for this loss class the filter is mathematically blind to targets. This retroactively reframes the prior project's "consensus on signal" interpretation: whatever helped on noisy-label MNIST, it cannot have been target-aware selection in any single-output regression setting.
  2. The filter amplifies rather than shrinks. The implicit hypothesis was regularisation-by-shrinkage — discarding noisy directions should make updates smaller and safer. Instead, on the MLP the filtered update was around 10× the size of the mean gradient on a large fraction of steps. It behaves like a rescaled projection, not a denoiser.
  3. Completely different internal behaviour, identical harm. On the MLP the filter kept ~1 direction and amplified heavily; on the GRU it kept ~60 directions with mild attenuation. Both produced near-identical degradation and the same "no better than random directions" null — strong evidence the effect is generic subspace projection, not anything spectral.
  4. The audit found a latent crash the original runs never hit: the eigendecomposition stochastically fails to converge in 32-bit floating point under certain seeds (the original 6-of-6 seed runs were simply lucky). A mathematically equivalent 64-bit fallback fixed it and reproduced all results; the defect is disclosed in the paper as an implementation-robustness issue, not a validity issue.

Limitations

  1. Small-scale, low-edge regime. To fit the free 30-minute GPU partition, the verdict used subsampled data (batch ≈1024, subsampled eras), and the evaluation-period baseline edge (+0.0064) was weaker than in the tuning period. Claims are explicitly scoped to this regime; a full-scale run (~$50–100 of cloud GPU) would test whether the harm persists at scale.
  2. Tuning asymmetry. The spectral arm got ~1 effective hyperparameter trial vs. the baseline's 12, which is why the headline claim is "no evidence of benefit under this budget" rather than "the method is harmful at its best configuration". The GRU arm also ran on a fallback configuration rather than a full re-tune.
  3. The escape route is known but untested. The target-independence proof applies only to single-output mean-squared-error losses. Multi-output, ranking, or correlation-aligned losses make the filter's selection target-dependent again — the mechanism could in principle work there, and this is the cheapest follow-up if the idea is worth rescuing.
